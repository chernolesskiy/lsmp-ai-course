So nobody tries everything. Instead the computer does what you would do on a mountain in thick fog: feel which way is downhill under your feet, take a small step, and feel again. With a billion knobs it nudges every knob at once, each a little in its own downhill direction, and repeats that millions of times. Face unlock, Google Translate and ChatGPT were all trained this way. The trick is called gradient descent, and it is today's lesson.

Field note

A marble in a bowl

Drop a marble in a bowl and it rolls to the bottom. Our walk is a very careful marble: it has no speed, it only looks at the slope where it stands. Real AI training often gives the walk a little speed, called momentum (an idea from 1964), so it can roll through small bumps instead of stopping in them.

Field note

How engineers choose the step size

Just like you: they test a few step sizes on a small problem first. Then they usually change it during training: small careful steps at the start, bigger steps for most of the journey, and smaller and smaller steps at the end so the walk settles at the bottom instead of zig-zagging.

Field note

Rough steps still get there

In 1951 two American mathematicians, Herbert Robbins and Sutton Monro, proved something surprising: a walk steered by rough, noisy measurements still reaches the bottom, as long as its steps shrink slowly enough. Today this stochastic gradient descent ("stochastic" means random) trains almost every AI, from phone keyboards to ChatGPT.

Field note

Rain on a ridge

Two raindrops land a few centimetres apart on a mountain ridge. One runs down to the left, the other to the right, and they end up in different seas. Where a walk starts can decide where it ends. Real AIs often start from many random places and keep the best result.

Check your understanding

You are standing on a hill. The slope under your feet is positive: the ground goes up to the right. Which way should you step to go downhill?

  1. To the right
  2. To the left
  3. Stay where you are
  4. Jump to a random place

Downhill is the opposite way to the slope. That is why the code does x = x - step_size * slope.

What happens when the step size (learning rate) is too big?

  1. It always finds the bottom faster
  2. It takes tiny steps and is very slow
  3. It overshoots: it zig-zags or flies off
  4. It gets stuck in the nearest small dip

Big steps overshoot the bottom. Too small is slow; too big zig-zags or flies away.

In Lesson 1 the computer tried thousands of lines. Why don't real AIs just try every combination of their numbers?

  1. Computers are not allowed to guess the answer
  2. Trying lines gives a less accurate answer
  3. Real AIs only have a few numbers to find
  4. There are far too many combinations to ever try them all

Real AIs have billions of numbers. Even 10 numbers with 100 values each is 10010 combinations. Walking downhill only needs the slope where you are now.

A real AI has a billion training examples. Why does each step use only a small random batch of them?

  1. A small batch gives the exact slope
  2. The computer can only remember ten examples
  3. A rough direction is good enough, so it can take far more steps for the same work
  4. Using all the examples would make the error go up

Like an opinion poll, a random handful points roughly the right way. Thousands of rough steps beat a few perfect ones.

What is a local minimum?

  1. The bottom of a dip that is not the lowest point overall
  2. The highest point of the valley
  3. A point where the slope is very steep
  4. The best possible answer

Walking downhill stops at the bottom of whatever dip you are in. It might not be the deepest one.
