AI on trial

Your group gets one case. Decide who is responsible and one rule you would make, and write them in the boxes. Use what you found today. One minute to present it.

Case 1

The AI detector

Surprise scores, false accusations

A school runs every essay through an AI detector. Sam's essay is flagged "97% likely AI". Sam wrote it himself; he writes in short, simple sentences. He gets zero and a letter home.

Who is responsible? Should a detector's score ever be enough on its own?

Case 2

The poisoned chatbot

Training data, the warm-up

A supermarket's help chatbot keeps learning from what customers type. For a week, a group of people tell it again and again that a rival shop's food is unsafe. Now it tells every customer that.

Who is responsible: the group, the supermarket, or the people who built it? How could it be protected?

Case 3

The homework helper

Hallucination

A student hands in an essay written by a chatbot. It quotes a book and a scientist that do not exist. The teacher fails the essay.

Who is to blame for the fake quotes? What should the rules on AI for homework be?

Case 4

Whose words?

Memory and copying

A company trains a chatbot on millions of books without asking the authors. It can now write "new" stories in the style of a living author, and sometimes repeats whole paragraphs of her books word for word.

Should the company have asked or paid? Who owns what the chatbot writes?

Case 5

The hiring robot

Bias in training data

A company uses AI to choose which job applicants get an interview. It learned from ten years of past hires, who were mostly men. Now it rejects most women.

Who is responsible? How could the company have caught this?

Case 6

The deepfake

Fake but convincing

A student uses a free app to make a fake video of a teacher saying something offensive. It is shared all over the school before anyone realises it is fake.

Who is responsible: the student, the app maker, or the people who shared it? What rule would you make?

Hallucination-proof facts

A model with a memory of 5 words stopped lying but copied everything. Can you keep the memory at 2 and still stop it lying, by changing how the facts are written? Keep all five facts, each city with its own country.

Hint
The model starts from the city's name and writes forwards, seeing only the last two words. After "capital of" it has seen five different countries, so it guesses. Keep each city first, and make sure the two words just before each country never come before a different country. How close to the city must the country be?

Real chatbots get facts wrong in the same places: where lots of different facts are written in the same words.

Check your understanding

What does a language model like ours actually predict?

  1. Whether a sentence is true or false
  2. The meaning of the whole book
  3. Which word is likely to come next
  4. Which book a sentence came from

It only predicts the next word, adds it, and repeats. Everything it writes is built one word at a time.

In the warm-up, a 60-word text hardly changed the story until the setting was large. Why?

  1. The book has hundreds of times more words, so its counts win unless yours are multiplied
  2. The model ignores any word it has not seen in the book
  3. Short texts are deleted before training
  4. The model only reads the first sentence of your text

The model just adds up counts. A 60-word text against a 26,680-word book is a tiny share, so it needs a big multiplier, or lots of copies. That is also why poisoning a real model takes many copies of the same lie.

With a memory of 5 words, the fact model never made anything up. Why is that not a good chatbot?

  1. It runs too slowly
  2. It forgets the start of the sentence
  3. It can only write about capital cities
  4. It copies what it read word for word, so it never writes anything new

Remembering more stopped the lies, but the book test showed it copying almost everything. A useful model has to write new sentences, and that is exactly where it can go wrong.

The detector worked when it flagged low surprise as AI. Why is model-written text unsurprising?

  1. Models only use short words
  2. A model picks likely next words, so each word is the kind the detector expects
  3. Models never use full stops
  4. The detector has read the model's sentences before

The generator picks words with high chances. The detector measures how high those chances were, so model text scores low. People choose odder words more often.

A detector gets 95 out of 100 right. Why is it unfair to fail a student on its score alone?

  1. Detectors are always wrong
  2. It only works on books
  3. About 1 in 20 is wrong, and people who write plainly are the ones it wrongly flags
  4. Students are allowed to use AI anyway

You wrote a real sentence that it called AI. Across a school, 95% means many innocent students flagged, and the ones who write simply are most at risk.
